## 3. Threat & Error Management (TEM)

<!-- Historical/heritage framing throughout — not a live planning brief. -->

### 3.1 CFIT / Terrain 🟥 (historical)
Kai Tak sat on the eastern side of Kowloon Bay in Kowloon, hemmed in by rugged terrain on three sides and water on the fourth. Less than 4 km north/northeast of the former RWY 13 threshold, a range of hills rose to roughly 2,000 ft (610 m); the hills east of the former RWY 31 threshold were less than 3 km away; and Hong Kong Island, immediately across Victoria Harbour to the south, carries hills up to roughly 2,100 ft (640 m). Dense high-rise development pressed directly against the northern airport boundary — at closure, six-storey buildings stood just across a major arterial road from the runway's northern end. This combination — close-in high terrain plus close-in urban structures — meant aircraft could not fly a straight-in instrument approach over the high ground to the northeast; instead traffic approached over Victoria Harbour and Kowloon City from the southwest (Wikipedia — Kai Tak Airport, retrieved 2026-07-26). This terrain and urban-density picture is retained here purely for heritage/TEM-literacy value; there is no current approach, missed-approach or departure procedure to apply it to.

### 3.2 Airborne conflict / traffic (historical) — N/A
Not applicable — closed aerodrome, no current traffic or ATC service. Historically, Kai Tak's single runway and manual-turn final approach meant traffic could not be sequenced as tightly as a modern radar-vectored field; procedural detail is not reconstructed here as it has no current relevance. Current-day airspace in the area is part of the Hong Kong (VHHK) FIR — see the [Hong Kong (VHHK) FIR brief](../../../../airspace/fir/asia/hong-kong-vhhk.md) and the [Asia airspace overview](../../../../airspace/asia.md) for the live picture.

### 3.3 Runway excursion (historical) — N/A
Not applicable — closed aerodrome, no current declared distances or braking data. Historically the single 13/31 runway was reclaimed land surrounded by water on three sides, so an excursion carried an above-average consequence profile; this is a heritage note, not an active operational consideration.

### 3.4 Weather threat 🟧 (historical)
The Runway 13 visual turn was demanding even in ordinary crosswinds, because the wind's effect on the aircraft changed through the 47° manual turn — a component that was a headwind on the inbound IGS course could become a destabilising crosswind through the turn. This was materially worse in the gusty, shifting winds thrown off the northeast hill range during typhoon conditions (Wikipedia — Kai Tak Airport, retrieved 2026-07-26). Hong Kong's South China Sea tropical cyclone season (broadly May–November, peaking July–September) is a regional climate fact that predates and postdates the airport; it has no current operational bearing on VHHX itself. For any live weather planning in the Hong Kong area, see the current [VHHH — Hong Kong Intl](../vhhh/index.md) briefing.

### 3.5 Operational considerations (historical) — N/A
Not applicable — closed aerodrome. Historically, RWY 13 was the preferred departure runway for heavy aircraft because it offered a clear departure path; a wind-driven runway change to RWY 31 could force a return to the terminal to offload payload, since the RWY 31 departure required a sharp 65° left turn shortly after take-off to clear the hill range (including Beacon Hill, ~1,500 ft/460 m) ahead of the runway (Wikipedia — Kai Tak Airport, retrieved 2026-07-26). Retained for heritage/TEM-literacy value only.

## 9. Navaids

Not applicable — closed aerodrome; all Kai Tak-era navaids were decommissioned at or shortly after closure. Historically, an **Instrument Guidance System (IGS)** was installed in 1974 to support the Runway 13 approach: a localizer-type aid offset by 47° from the runway centreline, used in conjunction with the visually-acquired **Checkerboard Hill** marking (see §10) — never a full ILS, since the approach could not be completed on instruments alone. Runway 31 was served by a conventional ILS during the operating era. None of this equipment exists today.

## 10. Arrival (historical reference only — never a current planning aid)

- **Transition altitude / level:** Not applicable — closed aerodrome.
- **Speed:** Not applicable — closed aerodrome.
- **Historical runway-use logic:** RWY 13 was used most of the time given prevailing local wind direction; RWY 31 was also used for early-morning landings for noise abatement (Wikipedia — Kai Tak Airport, retrieved 2026-07-26).
- **Historical approach description (heritage only — never fly this):**

| RWY | Historical approach | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 13 | **"Checkerboard" / IGS-13 approach** (visual only) | Historically, aircraft tracked westbound at a minimum altitude of roughly 6,500 ft, past Cheung Chau, to "Point Golf" south of Lantau Island, then flew a right-hand turn onto the IGS localizer (offset 47° from the runway). Autopilot was disconnected around 2,500 ft and the approach flown manually from there. On sighting the orange-and-white **Checkerboard Hill** marking near Kowloon Tsai Park, the crew made a visual right turn of approximately 47° at low altitude (historically entering the turn around 650 ft and rolling out around 140 ft) to align with the runway, roughly 2 NM from touchdown. ILS was never available for RWY 13 because of this mandatory visual turn (Wikipedia — Kai Tak Airport, retrieved 2026-07-26). |
| 31 | Conventional ILS approach (historical) | The reciprocal runway direction; approach path passed close to Hong Kong Island's northeastern shoreline. Used for early-morning noise-abatement landings. |

## 12. Noise & Environmental

- **Noise abatement (historical):** RWY 31 was used for early-morning landings specifically for noise abatement. Growing residential density around the field drove building-height restrictions in Kowloon during the operating era, lifted after closure.
- **Night noise / curfew (historical):** A night curfew of approximately **2330–0630 local** applied — a durable heritage fact, not a current restriction, since the aerodrome does not operate.

## 13. Ground operations

Not applicable in the operational sense — closed aerodrome, no current stands, taxiways or ground handling. For orientation only: the former airport site has been substantially redeveloped since closure — the **Kai Tak Cruise Terminal** opened in 2013 at the tip of the former runway; the **Kai Tak Sky Garden** (2021) occupies part of the former runway and apron; the **Kai Tak Sports Park** (opened March 2025, a roughly 50,000-seat stadium plus supporting venues) and a dedicated MTR **Kai Tak station** (opened 2020) sit on former airport land, alongside residential and hospital development (Wikipedia — Kai Tak Airport, retrieved 2026-07-26). This is heritage/site-context information only, useful for a scenery/heritage event, not a ground-operations reference.
