# A07 — Standard Operating Procedures — Philosophy and Structure · OM A

How the OM B/C/E procedural detail fits together under one SOP philosophy.

**Version** v1.0 · **Updated** 2026-07-25 · **Status** Verified

> Read-me: this chapter owns K Global's **SOP philosophy** and the **structure of the manual** — how policy, type, place and method relate, and the principles every procedural document applies. It sets the philosophy; the airborne flows and the FMS method are applied in OM E Flight Management and Operational Flows, and the challenge-and-response detail lives in the OM B airframe packs. ## 1. Purpose and the standardisation objective

Standard Operating Procedures exist to make the operation **predictable**. When both pilots operate to the same procedures, callouts and task-sharing, each can anticipate the other, cross-check effectively, and detect a deviation early. Standardisation is the foundation on which monitoring, threat management and safe automation use all depend.

- 🟥 **Comply with the SOP unless safety compels otherwise.** Crews operate to the published procedures. A degree of flexibility is essential in aircraft operations, and the commander retains discretion to use a non-standard practice to meet an unusual or unexpected situation **in the interest of safety** — but the SOP is the default, and a deliberate departure is a considered decision, not a habit.
- 🟩 **The commander may always apply greater margins.** Where the commander judges it necessary, greater safety margins (including aerodrome operating minima) may be applied. SOPs set the standard, not a ceiling on prudence.

## 2. How the manual fits together

The K Global OM is layered so that each fact lives in exactly one place and is applied — not repeated — elsewhere. Read top-down, a procedure is assembled from four layers:

| Layer | Manual | Answers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Policy** | **OM A** | *What we do* and the rules that govern it | This chapter; fuel policy (A08); rules of the air (A13) |
| **Type** | **OM B** | *How it is done on this airframe* | Per-type Checklist / QRH / Sim-Model / Dispatch |
| **Place** | **OM C** | *How it is done at this airport / in this airspace* | Aerodrome briefs, FIR/area briefs, route briefs |
| **Method** | **OM E** | *How it is done as a location-agnostic procedure* | Flight Management & Operational Flows; Fuel Policy; RVSM/PBN/Datalink |

- 🟩 **Policy is owned once, applied everywhere.** OM A states the *policy*; OM E turns policy into a repeatable *method*; OM B tailors the method to the airframe; OM C tailors it to the airport or airspace. When two layers touch the same subject they must stay **consistent** — the higher (policy) layer governs, and the lower layers apply it without contradiction.
- 🟥 **Type facts cannot be overridden by a general document.** The button-by-button sequence, the exact checklist items, autoflight logic and type limitations are **OM B facts**. This chapter and the OM E flow documents govern the *philosophy and structure*; they never override an airframe constraint.

## 3. SOP philosophy — the operating principles

### 3.1 PF / PM task-sharing

- 🟩 **Two defined roles.** At any time one pilot is **Pilot Flying (PF)** — controlling the flight path manually or through the autoflight system — and the other is **Pilot Monitoring (PM)**. In single-pilot sim operation the crew performs both roles; the discipline of the split is retained so that monitoring is a conscious act, not an afterthought.
- 🟥 **Monitoring is a primary task, not a spare one.** The PM's monitoring role is an **active** duty: cross-checking flight path, speed, configuration and mode, and calling deviations. The PF owns attitude, speed, configuration and the correct lateral/vertical modes for the phase; in the final part of a level capture, and on final approach, attention is concentrated on the task and monitoring must not break down. Handovers of control are explicit and acknowledged.

### 3.2 Standard callouts

- 🟩 **Standard phraseology, standard triggers.** Callouts are standardised so that the same event always produces the same call and the same expected response. They confirm mode and configuration changes, flag deviations, and drive the approach gates. A missing expected callout is itself a cue to cross-check.

### 3.3 Stabilised approach and go-around

- 🟥 **The stabilised-approach gate is a hard gate — not meeting it means go around.** An approach must be stabilised (on path, on speed, in the landing configuration, with thrust set and within the type's defined criteria) by the applicable gate. If it is not stabilised at the gate, or becomes unstable below it, the required action is an **immediate go-around** — a go-around is a normal, expected outcome, never a failure. Descent below minima without the required visual reference is prohibited (OM A13 / OM B).

### 3.4 Threat and Error Management (TEM)

- 🟩 **Anticipate, trap, mitigate.** SOPs are built around TEM: identify the **threats** for the sector (weather, terrain, airspace, airport, fatigue, automation), anticipate the **errors** they invite, and put the traps in place — through briefing, task-sharing, callouts and gates — before they become undesired aircraft states. The briefing is where threats are named; the flows and callouts are where they are trapped.

### 3.5 Automation-level management

- 🟩 **Use the right level of automation for the situation.** Automation is used to reduce workload and exposure to error, especially at high-workload times; the autoflight and autothrust/autothrottle are the normal means of flying the aircraft. But the crew **chooses the level** deliberately — full automation, selected modes, or manual flight — matching it to the task and workload, and always knows what the automation is doing and why. Manual flying is permitted after due consideration of workload and briefing; the PF must be ready to change automation level if workload or conditions change.

## 4. Where the philosophy is applied

- 🟩 **The airborne flows and FMS method** — the phase-by-phase operational flows, the FMS/MCDU management method, and the cross-check discipline that put this philosophy into practice — live in **OM E Flight Management and Operational Flows**. That document defines the *generic* flow; this chapter owns the *philosophy* it applies.
- 🟩 **The challenge-and-response detail** — the actual checklist items, the panel-scan sequence and every type-specific behaviour — lives in the **OM B airframe pack** (Checklist / QRH / Sim-Model). Checklists confirm that the flow has been completed correctly; they are not a substitute for the flow.
- 🟩 **The place layer** — aerodrome and airspace context for stabilised-approach/go-around, terrain and airspace threats — is applied per field and per region in the **OM C** briefs.
